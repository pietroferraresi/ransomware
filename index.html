Ransomware Attacks on
Critical Infrastructure

Policy Failures and a Path Forward

Pietro Ferraresi / Spring 2026 / Professor David Hoffman

Introduction

The Problem

Ransomware has evolved from low-level nuisance software, the kind that locked individual machines and demanded a few hundred dollars in gift cards, into one of the most pressing national security threats the United States faces today. Modern ransomware operations are run like businesses. They have customer service portals, affiliate programs, and negotiation teams. They target hospitals during surges in patient volume, pipeline operators during peak demand seasons, and payment processors that sit at the center of trillion-dollar transaction flows.

These attacks are not random. They are calculated assaults on the entities least able to tolerate downtime and most likely to pay. The operators behind them are sheltered in jurisdictions, primarily Russia and several former Soviet states, where they face little risk of prosecution as long as they avoid targeting domestic systems. The result is a threat environment in which critical services that Americans depend on every day are held hostage by criminal enterprises operating with near-impunity from abroad.

The United States designates sixteen critical infrastructure sectors whose assets, systems, and networks are considered so vital that their incapacitation would have a debilitating effect on national security, economic stability, public health, or safety. These sectors span everything from energy and water to healthcare, transportation, and financial services. Yet despite more than two decades of policy development since the first national cybersecurity strategy in 2003, the legal and regulatory framework meant to protect these sectors remains fragmented across dozens of agencies, largely voluntary in its application, and poorly enforced even where binding rules exist.

Fig 1. The Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) Ecosystem. Developers build and maintain the platform; affiliates carry out attacks; initial access brokers supply stolen credentials; crypto infrastructure launders payments.

This project examines that gap. Through three anchoring case studies, WannaCry (2017), Colonial Pipeline (2021), and Change Healthcare (2024), it traces the recurring policy failures that have left critical infrastructure exposed to an escalating threat. It then evaluates the current U.S. cybersecurity legal and policy framework, draws comparative insights from the United Kingdom's regulatory approach, and concludes with specific, actionable recommendations for reform.

Why has the United States struggled to deter and respond to ransomware attacks on critical infrastructure, and what reforms are needed?

Background

The 16 Critical Infrastructure Sectors

Presidential Policy Directive 21 (PPD-21), issued in 2013, identifies sixteen critical infrastructure sectors and assigns each a Sector Risk Management Agency (SRMA). The depth, enforceability, and resourcing of cybersecurity requirements varies enormously from one sector to the next. Some sectors operate under detailed binding regulations. Others have almost no enforceable federal cybersecurity requirements, leaving thousands of small utilities to fend for themselves.

Analysis

Why Critical Infrastructure Is Uniquely Vulnerable

Operational Constraints

A hospital ED cannot go offline for a week to rebuild its network. A pipeline operator supplying fuel to half the East Coast cannot pause deliveries. Attackers design their operations around this calculus. The urgency to restore operations creates pressure to pay ransoms quickly, funding future attacks in a self-reinforcing cycle.

Legacy Systems + IT/OT Convergence

Many CI sectors depend on aging OT never designed for internet connectivity. ICS, SCADA, and medical devices lack encryption, authentication, or patching capability. Over two decades, these systems have been connected to IT networks for monitoring and efficiency, creating pathways from a compromised email account directly into physical control systems.

Attribution + Deterrence Gaps

Ransomware groups operate from jurisdictions (primarily Russia) where they face minimal prosecution risk. The RaaS model fragments the ecosystem: developers, affiliates, and initial access brokers are separate entities. This makes attribution harder, legal action slower, and traditional deterrence frameworks poorly suited to the threat.

Information Asymmetries

Victims have strong incentives to conceal breaches. Before CIRCIA (still not fully implemented as of early 2026), the federal government had limited visibility into the scope and frequency of attacks. Without comprehensive data, it is impossible to design policy interventions that match the scale of the problem.

Technical Overview

WannaCry was a ransomware cryptoworm exploiting EternalBlue, a vulnerability in Microsoft's SMB protocol developed by the NSA's Tailored Access Operations (TAO) unit. Leaked by the Shadow Brokers in April 2017. Microsoft had patched it (MS17-010) two months before the attack, but vast numbers of systems remained unpatched, particularly those running end-of-life Windows XP. The worm propagated autonomously without user interaction, scanning for vulnerable SMB ports and self-replicating at extraordinary speed. Researcher Marcus Hutchins halted spread by registering a hardcoded kill-switch domain.

Impact: UK NHS

At least 80 of 236 hospital trusts affected across England and Scotland, along with 595 GP practices. Approximately 19,000 appointments cancelled, including urgent cancer referrals. Ambulances diverted, surgeries cancelled, some facilities turning away patients entirely. The National Audit Office later found the NHS had been warned repeatedly about cybersecurity vulnerabilities in the years before WannaCry but failed to act.

Impact: United States

Smaller direct impact, though Heritage Valley Health System in Pennsylvania reported disruptions to radiology, lab, and pharmacy systems. The more consequential American dimension: the NSA had discovered the SMB vulnerability and stockpiled it as an offensive weapon rather than disclosing it to Microsoft. When the Shadow Brokers leaked the exploit, it became globally available, transforming a classified intelligence asset into a destructive weapon accessible to anyone.

Policy Lessons

1. The VEP had prioritized offensive capability over defensive security. No transparency, no independent oversight, no accountability. 2. Patch management across critical infrastructure (especially healthcare) was dangerously inconsistent. The NHS ran thousands of machines on unsupported operating systems. 3. No international coordination mechanism existed for rapid ransomware response. Attribution to North Korea came months later; response was limited to statements and sanctions.

Fig 3. Colonial Pipeline attack chain: a single VPN credential without MFA led to shutdown of 45% of East Coast fuel supply.

Technical Overview

DarkSide, a RaaS operation based in Eastern Europe, used a compromised VPN credential without MFA. The attackers hit IT systems (billing, accounting), not OT directly. But Colonial could not verify OT was unaffected and could not operate the pipeline without billing infrastructure, so they shut down the entire 5,500-mile system as a precaution. The pipeline carries ~2.5 million barrels/day of gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and heating oil.

Impact

Fuel shortages across the southeastern U.S. Gas stations ran dry. Panic buying, price spikes. Virginia, North Carolina, Georgia, and Florida declared states of emergency. Jet fuel shortages at Charlotte Douglas and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta airports. Colonial paid $4.4M in Bitcoin; FBI recovered ~$2.3M by seizing DarkSide's crypto wallet. DarkSide announced shutdown shortly after, but successor groups (BlackMatter, ALPHV/BlackCat) emerged from its members.

Policy Lessons

Forced ransomware onto the national security agenda. EO 14028 and first-ever mandatory TSA pipeline cybersecurity directives followed within weeks. But before the attack, pipeline cybersecurity was voluntary. TSA had no enforcement mechanism. Colonial had not been reviewed in three years. The case also highlighted IT/OT interdependence: even without direct OT compromise, the inability to separate business and operational systems shut down physical infrastructure.

Fig 4. Change Healthcare attack chain. Same fundamental failure as Colonial Pipeline (no MFA on remote access), but with $3.1 billion in downstream costs.

Technical Overview

ALPHV/BlackCat affiliate gained access via a Citrix remote access portal without MFA (per UHG CEO Andrew Witty's Senate Finance Committee testimony). Spent 9 days inside the network exfiltrating data before deploying ransomware on February 21, 2024. UHG paid ~$22M in Bitcoin on March 3. The ALPHV/BlackCat leadership then took the payment and disappeared without paying the affiliate their share, who threatened additional data leaks through RansomHub.

Impact

Change Healthcare processes ~1 in 3 U.S. patient records. Pharmacies nationwide could not process insurance claims. Hospitals experienced weeks-long reimbursement delays. Cash flow crises especially for small/independent providers. 192.7 million individuals' data exposed (names, SSNs, diagnoses, treatment records, billing). Total cost to UHG: $3.09 billion through FY2024, excluding potential civil litigation from providers forced to downsize or close.

Policy Lessons

Concentration risk: one company's failure cascaded across the entire U.S. healthcare payment system. Existing frameworks focus on individual entities, not systemic interdependencies. Regulatory gap: HIPAA's Security Rule (last updated 2013) does not mandate MFA or specific technical controls. RaaS fragility: the affiliate scam shows the ecosystem's internal instability can be exploited by law enforcement through targeted disruption and trust erosion.

U.S. Policy Framework

A Patchwork of Authorities

Built incrementally over two decades. Broad in aspiration, shallow in enforcement, with critical gaps that ransomware operators have repeatedly exploited.

Timeline Five Gaps NIST CSF 2.0
Evolution

Key Policy Milestones

2003

HSPD-7 / National Strategy to Secure Cyberspace

First formal national cybersecurity strategy. Voluntary public-private partnerships, no enforcement. Identified CI protection as priority but provided no tools to achieve it.

2013

PPD-21 + EO 13636

Redesignated 16 CI sectors, assigned SRMAs. Directed NIST to develop the Cybersecurity Framework. Voluntary, non-binding, but became the most widely referenced standard globally.

2015

Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act

Legal protections (liability shields, FOIA exemptions) for voluntary threat indicator sharing. Many companies remained reluctant due to liability, competitive, and regulatory concerns.

2018

CISA Established

Former NPPD elevated to standalone DHS agency. Lead civilian cybersecurity coordination body. Can recommend, coordinate, assist. Cannot compel private sector action.

2021

Executive Order 14028

Post-Colonial Pipeline and SolarWinds. Zero-trust for federal agencies, SBOMs, EDR requirements, incident reporting to CISA. Primarily limited to government systems and contractors.

2022

CIRCIA Signed

First federal mandatory reporting: 72hr incidents, 24hr ransom payments. Delegated details to rulemaking. NPRM published April 2024, drew sharp industry criticism for breadth.

2023

National Cybersecurity Strategy

Called for shifting burden to providers/operators and mandatory minimum standards. Endorsed using regulatory authority more aggressively. Implementation uneven after administration change.

2025-26

CIRCIA Delays

Final rule pushed from October 2025 to May 2026. CISA conducting town hall sessions as of early 2026. DHS appropriations lapse disrupted even those town halls, making further delays likely. Four years after enactment, mandatory reporting still not operational.

Analysis

Where the Framework Falls Short

1. Voluntarism

The NIST CSF carries no legal obligation. Organizations can adopt it partially, selectively, or not at all. Financial services have binding GLBA requirements. Nuclear facilities have NRC rules. But healthcare relies on HIPAA (not updated since 2013, no specific technical mandates). Water systems (150,000+ public systems, mostly small) have almost no enforceable federal cybersecurity requirements at all.

2. Sector Fragmentation

16 sectors, 16 SRMAs, wildly different maturity levels and regulatory histories. No unified federal standard applies horizontally. TSA can now mandate pipeline cybersecurity but EPA lacks equivalent authority over water. National CI security is effectively set by the weakest sector, because attackers scan for and exploit whatever is most vulnerable regardless of which SRMA oversees it.

3. CIRCIA Implementation Delays

CIRCIA is genuine progress. But the NPRM drew 300,000+ entities into scope and provoked bipartisan criticism for exceeding congressional intent. Final rule pushed to May 2026 (at earliest) with additional town halls underway. DHS appropriations lapse disrupted March/April 2026 town halls. Four years after enactment, the government still lacks the comprehensive incident visibility CIRCIA was designed to provide.

4. Limited Deterrence

FBI infiltrated Hive for 7 months (providing decryption keys to 300+ victims) before seizing its infrastructure in January 2023. Seized ALPHV/BlackCat infrastructure in December 2023. But ALPHV reconstituted within weeks and hit Change Healthcare two months later. DarkSide rebranded as BlackMatter. New groups emerge rapidly from the same pool of experienced operators. Capability exists but is not applied with enough persistence to change the calculus.

5. CISA's Authority Gap

CISA's KEV catalog, Protective DNS, joint advisories, and incident response are widely respected. But CISA cannot require a hospital to patch a critical vulnerability, cannot mandate that a water utility implement MFA, cannot compel any private entity to take specific cybersecurity actions. Advisory-only. Effectiveness depends entirely on voluntary cooperation, which in cybersecurity has a well-documented track record of falling short.

Widely praised for flexibility and comprehensiveness. Adopted globally by regulators, auditors, insurers. But flexibility is the core limitation for CI: organizations retain wide latitude to define "appropriate" implementation. A healthcare system can claim NIST CSF alignment while still lacking MFA, running unpatched systems, and having no tested backup plan. The framework describes what good cybersecurity looks like. It does not require it.

Comparative Analysis

The UK Approach

Centralized coordination, mandatory standards, active operational defence. Not directly transplantable but addresses the specific structural gaps exposed by WannaCry, Colonial Pipeline, and Change Healthcare.

Institutional Design

The National Cyber Security Centre

Established October 2016 as a publicly facing branch of GCHQ (UK signals intelligence). This is deliberate: the lead cybersecurity coordination body gets direct, real-time access to intelligence while simultaneously serving as the government's primary interface with the private sector on defensive cybersecurity.

The NCSC provides threat assessments, manages national incident response, publishes actionable technical guidance, and operates the Active Cyber Defence (ACD) program: automated tools that proactively block malicious activity at national scale. ACD includes Protective DNS (blocks known malicious domains), takedown services (removes phishing sites and malware on UK infrastructure), and Mail Check (deploys DMARC/SPF/DKIM email authentication).

Fig 6. U.S. vs. UK institutional architecture for cybersecurity. The U.S. distributes responsibilities across multiple agencies; the UK centralizes them in the NCSC.
Regulatory Framework

NIS Regulations + Cyber Assessment Framework

The NIS Regulations (2018, updated 2022) impose mandatory cybersecurity on operators of essential services in energy, transport, health, water, and digital infrastructure. Competent authorities (Ofgem, Ofcom, DHSC) can inspect, issue enforcement notices, and impose fines up to £17 million. The 2022 update added managed service providers to scope, recognizing supply chain risk.

The NCSC's Cyber Assessment Framework (CAF) provides the assessment methodology. Four objectives: managing security risk, protecting against attack, detecting events, minimising impact. Explicitly outcomes-based: does not mandate specific technologies, requires demonstrated security outcomes. Preserves flexibility while establishing an auditable, enforceable baseline.

Takeaways

What Translates

Centralization reduces friction. The NCSC's single-authority model eliminates inter-agency coordination problems. The U.S. could strengthen CISA's mandate without replicating GCHQ's structure.

Mandatory baselines are achievable. NIS proves enforceable standards work without stifling innovation. The CAF's outcomes approach could adapt to U.S. sectors under voluntary frameworks.

Active defence at scale works. ACD has blocked millions of malicious emails and taken down hundreds of thousands of phishing sites annually. The U.S. has the capability but has not deployed it at comparable scale.

What doesn't translate directly: the U.S. is vastly larger, has deeper political resistance to centralized regulation, and maintains an intelligence/civilian separation that reflects legitimate civil liberties concerns. But the specific capabilities (mandatory baselines, active defence, coordinating authority) are adaptable without replicating the UK's institutional structure.

Recommendations

A Path Forward

The same failures recur across all three cases. Six recommendations, each grounded in the case study evidence and the comparative UK analysis.

REC 01

Mandatory Standards

Outcomes-based baselines for all 16 CI sectors.

REC 02

Accelerate CIRCIA

Finalize rulemaking. Close the reporting gap.

REC 03

Strengthen CISA

Binding directives during active incidents.

REC 04

Concentration Risk

"Systemically important" designations for digital CI.

REC 05

Active Defence

National-scale ACD + sustained ransomware disruption.

REC 06

Reform VEP

Legislate. Independent review. Default to disclosure.

Rec 01

Mandate Minimum Standards for All CI Sectors

Congress should require all 16 sectors to meet enforceable, outcomes-based baselines modeled on the UK's CAF. At minimum: MFA on all remote access and privileged accounts; timely patching per CISA's KEV catalog; IT/OT segmentation; encrypted, tested backups; annually reviewed IR plan; basic supply chain risk management.

SRMAs must be empowered and funded to assess compliance with graduated penalties. The current asymmetry (TSA mandates for pipelines but EPA cannot for water, HHS cannot require hospitals to implement MFA) is exploitable. For small, resource-constrained entities, pair mandates with federal grants and technical assistance.

The question is no longer whether mandatory standards are appropriate for critical infrastructure. The case studies settled that debate. The question is how to design them: enforceable, proportionate, adaptable.

Rec 02

Accelerate CIRCIA Implementation

Four years between enactment and projected implementation is too long for an active threat. Final rule needs clear definitions, minimal ambiguity, strong legal protections for candid disclosure. Extend mandatory reporting to ransomware payments in a centralized database accessible to law enforcement, intelligence, and Treasury/OFAC. Comprehensive payment flow data is essential for disrupting the crypto infrastructure that sustains the ransomware economy.

Rec 03

Strengthen CISA's Coordinating Authority

Grant CISA authority to issue binding operational directives to CI operators during active incidents (analogous to existing BOD authority over federal agencies). Empower CISA to conduct compliance assessments and refer persistent non-compliance to sector regulators. Not a traditional regulator, but no longer purely advisory.

Rec 04

Address Systemic Concentration Risk

Develop "systemically important" designations for digital infrastructure entities, analogous to SIFIs in banking post-2008. Change Healthcare processed ~1/3 of all U.S. health transactions. Colonial Pipeline carried 45% of East Coast fuel. These are not ordinary companies with ordinary risk profiles. Heightened requirements: mandatory resilience testing, redundancy planning, enhanced IR obligations, potentially structural requirements around processing redundancy.

Rec 05

Active Defence + Sustained Disruption

Build national-scale ACD modeled on NCSC: proactive blocking of malicious domains, C2 infrastructure, phishing at network level. CISA's Protective DNS and JCDC are foundations but not at UK scale. Simultaneously, DOJ/FBI must intensify sustained disruption. The Hive infiltration (7 months inside, 300+ victims helped) shows what patience achieves. The Joint Ransomware Task Force needs permanent resourcing, staffing, and mandate for continuous operational pressure.

Rec 06

Reform the Vulnerabilities Equities Process

Congress should legislate the VEP. Statutory criteria for disclosure decisions. Independent review mechanism (PCLOB or similar). Regular classified reporting to intelligence committees on volume and disposition of vulnerabilities retained. Default presumption: disclosure. Offensive value degrades as systems are upgraded. Defensive risk persists as long as vulnerable systems remain in operation. WannaCry was a direct consequence of getting this balance wrong.

Ransomware is not an unsolvable problem. It is a policy failure, one that persists because costs distribute across millions of victims while reform requires concentrated political will. The tools exist. What is missing is the sustained commitment to use them.
