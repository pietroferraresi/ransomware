COMPSCI 255 // Cyber Policy

Ransomware Attacks on Critical Infrastructure

Policy Failures and a Path Forward

Pietro Ferraresi // 2026

Introduction

The Problem

In the early 2010s, the nature of ransomware was relatively simple. It encrypted files, asked for hundreds of dollars in bitcoin as payment, and left. This is no longer the case. Modern ransomware operations function similarly to organized crime on an enterprise level. Ransomware criminals run affiliate programs, provide victims with help desks, and employ professional negotiators. Furthermore, the target selection of such groups is based on entities which simply cannot afford downtime like hospitals treating patients, pipelines transporting fuel to a large number of consumers, and payment processors which process a majority of the nation's health care transactions.

None of this happens by chance. Modern ransomware criminal enterprises are deliberate and precise in their choice of victim organizations. They pursue targets where the cost of downtime is high enough to warrant paying millions of dollars as ransom. Such criminal operations usually reside in other countries, namely Russia, where they can safely conduct their operations as long as they do not target entities within their own jurisdictions.

According to the US government, there are sixteen critical infrastructure sectors which are crucial to the national security and the wellbeing of its citizens. Unfortunately, after two decades of cybersecurity policy initiatives, the framework for protecting such sectors is severely flawed. Protection responsibilities are scattered among many different agencies. Most requirements are purely voluntary. Where there are mandatory rules, there is little enforcement of them.

How ransomware-as-a-service works.

This site looks at how we got here. Three case studies (WannaCry, Colonial Pipeline, and Change Healthcare) show the same failures playing out again and again across seven years. From there, it breaks down the current U.S. policy framework and its gaps, compares it to what the UK has built, and ends with six concrete recommendations for what should change.

Why does the U.S. keep getting hit by ransomware attacks on critical infrastructure, and what actually needs to change?

Background

The 16 Critical Infrastructure Sectors

Presidential Policy Directive 21 (2013) identifies sixteen sectors and assigns each a lead federal agency called a Sector Risk Management Agency (SRMA). In practice, the security requirements across these sectors range from genuinely rigorous (financial services, nuclear) to essentially nonexistent (water systems, many healthcare providers).

Vulnerability

Why Critical Infrastructure Keeps Getting Hit

Can't Afford Downtime

A hospital’s emergency room does not have the ability to shut down its operations for a week while undergoing an overhaul of its computer networks. In the same vein, a pipeline that provides fuel to half the population on the East Coast cannot stop operating while an assessment is being made of its control systems for any weaknesses that might be exploited. Ransomware operators are quite well versed with such relationships and take advantage of them.

Ancient Systems, New Connections

There is a significant number of critical infrastructure components that function based on technology which was originally created without consideration of its use in networking. Reliability, as opposed to security, was a key requirement for development of industrial control systems, SCADA systems, and medical equipment. Within the last couple of decades, these devices have become increasingly integrated into company networks in order to enhance their functionality. Through this integration, hackers have found themselves able to access the process-control systems via common points like a compromised email account.

Safe Havens

The leading ransomware groups typically operate out of Russia and nearby areas, where law enforcement is less likely to intervene unless the group is attacking local organizations. The use of the RaaS model makes the problem even worse by making the process of creating, operating, and brokering access codes through which data is stolen more fragmented and therefore more difficult to attribute.

Nobody Knows the Full Picture

Organizations that suffer attacks will have significant motives for concealing this information. Disclosures could result in falling stock prices, lawsuits, more stringent regulatory oversight, and even loss of reputation. Until such mandated disclosures become reality as envisioned by CIRCIA (which had not been done as of early 2026), the government remains blind to the occurrence or extent of such cyberattacks.

Three Incidents, One Pattern

What Happened

WannaCry was a ransomware worm that exploited a vulnerability in Windows called EternalBlue. Here is the part that matters for policy: EternalBlue was discovered by the NSA, which decided to keep it secret and use it as a hacking tool rather than telling Microsoft about it so it could be patched. When a group called the Shadow Brokers leaked the exploit in April 2017, it became available to anyone. Microsoft had actually released a patch (MS17-010) in March 2017, two months before the attack, but enormous numbers of systems were still running unpatched, especially legacy Windows XP machines in hospitals and industrial settings that could not easily be upgraded.

The worm spread on its own without anyone needing to click anything. Once it hit one machine on a network, it scanned for other vulnerable systems and replicated itself. A security researcher named Marcus Hutchins accidentally found a kill switch by registering a domain that was hardcoded into the malware. That slowed the spread, but machines that were already encrypted stayed locked.

The Damage

The worst impact was on the UK's National Health Service. Over 80 hospital trusts were hit, along with 595 GP practices. About 19,000 appointments got cancelled, including cancer referrals. Ambulances were rerouted and some hospitals turned patients away. The UK's National Audit Office later found that the NHS had been warned about its cybersecurity problems multiple times before WannaCry and had done essentially nothing about it.

In the U.S., a few hospitals including Heritage Valley Health System in Pennsylvania had disruptions, but the bigger issue was the origin of the exploit. The NSA had found this vulnerability and sat on it for years instead of getting it fixed. When it leaked, the agency's offensive weapon became everyone's weapon.

What It Showed

Three things. First, the Vulnerabilities Equities Process (the system for deciding whether the government discloses or keeps vulnerabilities) was broken, with no transparency or accountability. Second, patch management in critical infrastructure, especially healthcare, was in terrible shape. Third, there was no real mechanism for countries to coordinate a rapid response to a ransomware attack that was hitting 150+ nations simultaneously.

What Happened

A group called DarkSide, running a ransomware-as-a-service operation out of Eastern Europe, got into Colonial Pipeline's network using a single compromised VPN password. The account did not have multi-factor authentication turned on. Once inside, they moved through Colonial's IT systems and encrypted billing and business infrastructure.

Here is the critical detail: DarkSide never actually touched the pipeline's control systems. They hit IT, not OT. But Colonial could not verify whether the operational technology was also compromised, and they could not run the pipeline without the billing systems that track fuel deliveries. So they shut the entire thing down as a precaution. A compromised password for a VPN with no MFA ended up taking offline the pipeline that carries 45% of the East Coast's fuel.

The Damage

Fuel shortages across the southeast. Gas stations running dry. Panic buying. Price spikes. Multiple states declaring emergencies. Jet fuel shortages at Charlotte and Atlanta airports. Colonial paid $4.4 million in Bitcoin. The FBI later managed to recover about $2.3 million of that by seizing DarkSide's crypto wallet, which was a rare win. DarkSide announced it was shutting down shortly after, but its members later showed up in successor groups like BlackMatter and ALPHV/BlackCat.

What It Showed

This was the incident that put ransomware on the White House's radar as a national security issue. Executive Order 14028 and TSA's first-ever mandatory pipeline cybersecurity directives came within weeks. But the deeper lesson was that pipeline cybersecurity had been completely voluntary before this. TSA had no enforcement power. Colonial had not been reviewed in three years. And the IT/OT convergence problem (where taking down business systems also takes down physical operations) turned out to be endemic across virtually every CI sector.

What Happened

Change Healthcare is a subsidiary of UnitedHealth Group that processes roughly one out of every three U.S. healthcare transactions: insurance claims, payments, prior authorizations, the works. An affiliate of the ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware group got in through a Citrix remote access portal that did not have multi-factor authentication. That is the same basic failure as Colonial Pipeline, three years later.

The attackers spent nine days inside the network stealing data before deploying ransomware on February 21, 2024. UnitedHealth paid about $22 million in Bitcoin on March 3. Then things got even messier: the ALPHV/BlackCat leadership apparently took the money and disappeared without paying the affiliate their cut. The affiliate then threatened to leak the stolen data through a different group called RansomHub, essentially trying to extort UnitedHealth a second time.

The Damage

Pharmacies across the country could not process insurance claims. Hospitals went weeks without getting reimbursed for services they had already provided. Small and independent practices, community health centers, and rural hospitals got hit the hardest because they did not have the cash reserves to survive weeks without revenue. About 192.7 million people had their personal health data exposed, making it the largest healthcare breach in U.S. history. UnitedHealth's total cost reached $3.09 billion for fiscal year 2024 alone.

What It Showed

This one was about concentration risk. Change Healthcare is so embedded in the U.S. healthcare payment system that when it went down, the entire system seized up. Current regulations do not account for that kind of systemic dependency. HIPAA's security rule has not been meaningfully updated since 2013 and does not require MFA or any other specific technical control. And the ALPHV affiliate drama showed that the RaaS ecosystem is internally fragile in ways that law enforcement and intelligence agencies could exploit more aggressively.

U.S. Framework

A Patchwork of Authorities

Twenty years of cybersecurity policy, built crisis by crisis, with critical gaps that ransomware operators keep exploiting.

Timeline

How We Got Here

2003

HSPD-7

First national cybersecurity strategy. Identified CI protection as a priority but gave nobody the authority to actually require anything. Voluntary partnerships only.

2013

PPD-21 + EO 13636

Designated the 16 CI sectors and told NIST to build a Cybersecurity Framework. NIST CSF became the most widely used cybersecurity reference standard in the world, but it was always voluntary.

2015

Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act

Created legal protections for companies that share threat data with the government. Good idea in theory, but voluntary participation meant uptake was limited.

2018

CISA Created

DHS stood up a dedicated cybersecurity agency. CISA became the main coordinating body for civilian cybersecurity, but its powers are advisory. It can warn, help, and coordinate. It cannot compel.

2021

EO 14028

The Colonial Pipeline response. Zero-trust mandates, software supply chain requirements, incident reporting. Significant but mostly applies to federal systems and government contractors, not private CI broadly.

2022

CIRCIA

First mandatory reporting law: 72 hours for incidents, 24 hours for ransom payments. But the details got punted to rulemaking, and that rulemaking has been delayed repeatedly.

2025-26

CIRCIA Still Not Done

Final rule pushed to May 2026. CISA is running town halls in early 2026 to gather more industry input. A DHS funding lapse disrupted even those. Four years after the law passed, it still is not operational.

Analysis

The Five Gaps

1. Voluntary means optional

NIST CSF is a good standard. The problem is that nobody has to follow it. Financial services have binding GLBA requirements. Nuclear facilities have NRC rules. But healthcare relies on HIPAA, which has not been meaningfully updated since 2013 and does not mandate anything as basic as multi-factor authentication. The water sector, with over 150,000 public systems (most of them tiny), has almost no enforceable federal cybersecurity requirements at all.

2. Fragmentation across agencies

Sixteen sectors, sixteen lead agencies, wildly different levels of maturity and regulatory authority. TSA can now mandate cybersecurity for pipelines (because Colonial Pipeline forced the issue), but EPA cannot do the same for water systems and HHS cannot require hospitals to turn on MFA. The weakest sector sets the bar for the whole country, because attackers will always go after whatever is easiest to hit.

3. CIRCIA is years late

CIRCIA was a real step forward. But four years between signing the law and having it actually take effect is too long when the threat is active and evolving. The NPRM drew criticism for trying to cover 300,000+ entities, the final rule got pushed from October 2025 to May 2026, and a DHS funding lapse has disrupted even the most recent round of industry engagement. In the meantime, the government still does not have systematic visibility into how often critical infrastructure is getting hit.

4. Disruption without persistence

The FBI infiltrated the Hive network for seven months before taking it down in January 2023, helping over 300 victims with decryption keys along the way. That was impressive. They also seized ALPHV/BlackCat infrastructure in December 2023. But ALPHV reconstituted within weeks and hit Change Healthcare two months later. Technical capability is there. What is missing is sustained, persistent pressure that actually changes the economics for these groups.

5. CISA cannot compel anyone

CISA does solid work: their KEV catalog, Protective DNS service, advisories, and incident response teams are well-regarded. But if CISA identifies a critical vulnerability being actively exploited across the healthcare sector, it can issue a warning. It cannot require anyone to actually patch. That gap between knowing what is wrong and being able to fix it is a fundamental structural problem.

Framework

NIST CSF 2.0

Updated in February 2024, the framework organizes cybersecurity into six functions. Version 2.0 added Govern at the center to emphasize that leadership and risk management are foundational.

NIST CSF 2.0 core functions.

The framework is well-built and widely adopted. But for critical infrastructure, the flexibility that makes it useful also makes it toothless: an organization can claim alignment with NIST CSF while still running unpatched systems, having no MFA on remote access, and never testing its backups. The framework says what good looks like. It does not make anyone do it.

UK Comparison

What the UK Built Instead

A centralized agency, mandatory standards, and active defense at national scale. Not perfect, but it addresses the specific gaps that keep showing up in the U.S. case studies.

The NCSC

One Agency, Not Seven

The UK set up the National Cyber Security Centre in 2016 as a public-facing arm of GCHQ (their signals intelligence agency). That placement matters: the country's lead cybersecurity body has direct access to intelligence capabilities while also being the main point of contact for businesses, hospitals, and everyone else on defensive cybersecurity.

The NCSC runs the Active Cyber Defence program, which automatically blocks known malicious domains, takes down phishing sites hosted on UK infrastructure, and deploys email authentication standards at national scale. These are not just advisories. They are operational tools running across UK networks, providing a baseline layer of defense that individual organizations do not have to build themselves.

Compare that to the U.S., where the same set of responsibilities gets split across CISA, NSA, FBI, Secret Service, and a dozen sector-specific regulators, each with different authorities, budgets, classification levels, and institutional priorities.

U.S. vs. UK institutional structure.
Standards

NIS Regulations + the CAF

The UK's NIS Regulations (2018, updated 2022) require operators of essential services in energy, transport, health, water, and digital infrastructure to meet mandatory cybersecurity standards. Sector regulators like Ofgem and Ofcom can inspect, issue enforcement notices, and fine organizations up to 17 million pounds.

The assessment tool is the Cyber Assessment Framework (CAF), developed by the NCSC. It is outcomes-based: instead of telling organizations which specific software to buy, it requires them to demonstrate that they are achieving defined security outcomes. This keeps things flexible without making compliance optional.

Takeaways

What Actually Translates

The UK model cannot be copy-pasted onto the U.S. The country is smaller, the politics around regulation are different, and housing a civilian cybersecurity agency inside a signals intelligence service raises civil liberties issues that do not apply the same way here. But several specific elements address exactly the weaknesses exposed in the three case studies:

Mandatory baselines are doable. The NIS Regulations show you can impose enforceable standards on critical infrastructure without killing innovation. The CAF's outcomes-based approach is a template that could work in U.S. sectors currently operating under voluntary frameworks.

Active defense at scale works. The ACD program blocks millions of malicious emails and takes down hundreds of thousands of phishing sites a year. The U.S. has the technical ability to do this but has not deployed it at anything close to the same scale.

One authority is faster than seven. When the NCSC identifies a threat, the response is immediate because everything sits in the same organization. In the U.S., the same process involves coordination across CISA, NSA, FBI, and whichever sector regulator happens to be relevant, with all the friction that implies.

Recommendations

Six Things That Should Change

Each one is grounded in the case studies and the UK comparison. None of them require inventing new technology. They require political will.

01

Mandatory Minimum Standards for Every CI Sector

Congress should require all sixteen sectors to meet enforceable, outcomes-based cybersecurity baselines modeled on the UK's CAF. At minimum: MFA on all remote access, patching of CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities within defined timeframes, IT/OT network segmentation, encrypted and tested backups, and an annually exercised incident response plan.

The agencies responsible for each sector need the authority and funding to actually check compliance and impose penalties when organizations fall short. Right now TSA can mandate pipeline security but EPA cannot do the same for water and HHS cannot require hospitals to use MFA. For smaller organizations that genuinely lack resources, pair the requirements with federal grants and technical assistance. But the baseline itself should not be optional.

Whether mandatory standards are appropriate for critical infrastructure is not really a question anymore. The case studies settled it. The real question is how to design them so they are proportionate, enforceable, and adaptable across sixteen very different sectors.

02

Finish CIRCIA

Four years between passing a law and having it take effect is not acceptable when the threat it was designed to address is actively getting worse. CISA needs to finalize the rulemaking with clear definitions, workable scope, and strong legal protections so that companies actually report honestly rather than treating compliance as a liability exercise. The reporting data also needs to cover ransom payments specifically, in a centralized database that law enforcement and Treasury can use to trace and disrupt the financial flows keeping the ransomware economy alive.

03

Give CISA Real Authority

CISA should be able to issue binding operational directives to critical infrastructure operators during active incidents, the same way it already can for federal agencies. If there is a vulnerability being actively exploited across the healthcare sector right now, CISA should be able to require patching, not just suggest it. This does not mean turning CISA into a traditional regulator for every sector. It means giving it emergency powers and the ability to refer persistent non-compliance to the relevant sector agency for enforcement.

04

Designate Systemically Important CI Entities

Change Healthcare processed about a third of all U.S. healthcare transactions. Colonial Pipeline carried 45% of East Coast fuel. When entities like that go down, entire sectors seize up. Current regulations treat every organization the same and do not account for systemic concentration risk. We need something like the SIFI designation from banking after 2008: identify the entities whose failure would cascade across a sector, and subject them to heightened requirements around resilience testing, redundancy, and incident response. These are not ordinary companies and should not be regulated as though they are.

05

Build Active Defense at Scale

The UK's ACD program blocks malicious domains, takes down phishing sites, and deploys email authentication across the whole country, mostly automatically. The U.S. has the technical ability to do this. CISA's Protective DNS is a start, but it is nowhere near UK scale. Build it out. At the same time, keep up the offensive disruption. The FBI's Hive operation (seven months inside, 300+ victims helped) showed what patient, sustained disruption can accomplish. Fund the Joint Ransomware Task Force permanently and give it the mandate to maintain continuous pressure.

06

Legislate the VEP

WannaCry happened in large part because the NSA found a vulnerability and decided to keep it as a weapon instead of telling Microsoft so it could be patched. The Vulnerabilities Equities Process, which is supposed to govern that tradeoff, has no statutory basis, no independent oversight, and no public accountability. Congress should put it in law with clear criteria for when disclosure should happen (default: disclose), an independent review mechanism, and regular reporting to the intelligence committees. Offensive value degrades over time as systems get upgraded. Defensive risk persists as long as vulnerable systems are still running.

Ransomware is not some unstoppable force of nature. It is a policy failure. The costs get spread across millions of victims while fixing it requires the kind of concentrated political effort that is hard to sustain. But the tools exist. The knowledge exists. What has been missing, consistently, is the will to actually use them.
