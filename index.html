COMPSCI 255 // Cyber Policy

Ransomware Attacks on Critical Infrastructure

Current policy failures and a path forward

Pietro Ferraresi // 2026

Introduction

The Problem

In the early 2010s, ransomware was quite tame. It encrypted your personal files, demanded a few hundred dollars in Bitcoin, and you either paid or restored from a backup. The damage was real but contained. By 2024, that model is unrecognizable. Today's ransomware operations run like legitimate software companies. They have affiliate programs with revenue-share agreements, around-the-clock customer support for victims trying to negotiate, professional media relations teams, and internal dispute resolution processes. ALPHV/BlackCat, the group behind the Change Healthcare attack, even had an internal arbitration board to handle disputes between affiliates and the core operators.

The shift that made all of this profitable was the decision to stop going after individuals and start going after organisations that literally cannot afford to be offline. A hospital running at 105% capacity during flu season cannot negotiate for three weeks while IT rebuilds the network. A pipeline moving 2.5 million barrels a day of gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel from Houston to New Jersey cannot verify whether its operational technology has been touched without bringing the whole system down. A payment processor that sits between one in every three American patients and their insurance company cannot just pause billing for a month. These are the targets, and the attackers know exactly what an hour of downtime costs each of them.

How ransomware-as-a-service works.

On the defensive side, the US government has sixteen officially designated critical infrastructure sectors under Presidential Policy Directive 21 (2013), which was later updated and largely replaced by National Security Memorandum 22 (2024). These include energy, healthcare, water, transportation, financial services, and communications, among others. The premise is that if any of these went down simultaneously, the country would be in real trouble. The problem is that the rules protecting them are fragmented across dozens of agencies, largely voluntary, and poorly enforced where mandatory rules do exist. The same basic failure, missing multi-factor authentication on a remote access portal, took down Colonial Pipeline in 2021 and then Change Healthcare in 2024. Three years apart, same category of mistake, and in between those two attacks the federal government passed a major cyber reporting law (CIRCIA) that still is not operational as of early 2026.

This site examines three case studies in detail, explains how the US policy framework currently works and where it breaks down, compares it to the United Kingdom's approach (centralised, mandatory, enforcement-backed), and ends with six specific recommendations grounded in what the case studies actually show.

Why does the US keep getting hit by ransomware attacks on critical infrastructure, and what actually needs to change?

The 16 Sectors

The 16 Critical Infrastructure Sectors

PPD-21 (February 2013) originally identified the sixteen sectors and assigned each one a lead federal agency called a Sector Risk Management Agency (SRMA). That designation was updated and reaffirmed under National Security Memorandum 22 (April 2024), which modernised the framework but kept the same sixteen sectors. In practice, the security requirements across these sectors range from genuinely rigorous (financial services under Gramm-Leach-Bliley, nuclear under the Nuclear Regulatory Commission) to essentially nonexistent (water and wastewater systems, where over 150,000 public utilities exist with almost no federal cybersecurity requirements).

Vulnerability

Why Critical Infrastructure Keeps Getting Hit

Can't Afford Downtime

A hospital emergency room cannot stop operating for a week while IT rebuilds the network. A pipeline carrying 45% of East Coast fuel cannot shut down while engineers audit every system. A payment processor handling one in three US patient records cannot pause while forensics run. Ransomware operators know these relationships precisely and price their demands to a fraction of what downtime actually costs. At Colonial, the $4.4 million ransom was a tiny number against the billions in lost economic activity the shutdown was about to cause. UnitedHealth's $22 million ransom on Change Healthcare worked out to roughly 0.7% of the $3.09 billion the breach would eventually cost them. Both companies made what they viewed as the rational business choice, and both payments funded the next wave of attacks.

Old Systems, New Connections

A large portion of critical infrastructure runs on technology that predates the modern internet. Industrial control systems (ICS), SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) platforms, and medical devices were engineered for reliability and safety, not confidentiality or authentication. Many of them do not support encryption, cannot handle modern authentication protocols, and receive patches irregularly if at all. Over the last twenty years, these systems have been connected to corporate IT networks for monitoring and efficiency, which means an attacker who compromises an HR email account can sometimes pivot into the network segment that controls pumps and valves. This is the IT/OT convergence problem and it is what made Colonial's shutdown so severe: DarkSide never directly touched operational technology, but Colonial could not confirm OT was clean and could not run billing either, so they shut everything down as a precaution.

What Happened

The self-replicating WannaCry ransomware attack was carried out by exploiting a vulnerability in Microsoft's Server Message Block protocol (SMBv1), which is commonly known as EternalBlue. This vulnerability was discovered by the NSA's Tailored Access Operations Unit, likely in 2013. Following their discovery of the vulnerability, the NSA classified it as an offensive capability and chose not to disclose the vulnerability to Microsoft in order to patch it but rather decided to retain it for future use in intelligence operations. This decision by the NSA lies at the heart of why WannaCry happened.

On May 12, 2017, WannaCry began spreading. The worm infected systems in over 150 countries within hours of becoming operational due to its technical design, which required no user interaction. Traditional ransomware spreads when a user interacts with something and clicks something, whereas WannaCry utilised the EternalBlue worm to move laterally and replicate across entire networks. All it took was one machine to become infected, and all others on the network were soon after compromised without users being involved in any way. The spread continued for seven hours until an incident occurred which stopped WannaCry in its tracks. Marcus Hutchins, a 22-year-old British cybersecurity researcher, noticed that the malware would check for the existence of a specific domain prior to execution. For $10, Hutchins registered the domain, which became a kill-switch that stopped infections dead in their tracks.

The Damage in the UK

Perhaps the greatest organisational effect was experienced by the United Kingdom's National Health Service. According to the National Audit Office's report of WannaCry (October 2017), at least 80 out of 236 NHS trust providers in England were affected, as well as 603 primary care and other NHS organisations, including 595 GP surgeries. Some 19,000 appointments, including urgent cancer referrals, were canceled as a result. Five emergency departments had to reroute ambulances to other hospitals since they were unable to take any additional patients. The tone used in the NAO report was clear and unambiguous, with the statement that "the NHS had been warned of the risk of a cyber-attack on the NHS a year before WannaCry and although it had work ongoing, it did not formally respond to the warning until July 2017." The Department of Health and NHS England did not ensure that NHS organisations patched their systems and responded to CareCERT alerts. This attack could have been completely prevented, and its occurrence in such a situation was what made it so damning for the voluntary guidance method.

The cyber-attack could have caused more disruption if it had not been stopped by a cyber researcher activating a 'kill switch' so that WannaCry stopped locking devices... The Department has no formal mechanism for assessing whether NHS organisations have complied with its advice and guidance. — UK National Audit Office, "Investigation: WannaCry Cyber Attack and the NHS," October 2017

What It Showed

The two things that would still exist seven years later in the Change Healthcare case. Firstly, the Vulnerabilities Equities Process, which should provide guidance on whether the vulnerability will be disclosed by the US government or used for offensive purposes, was running without any statute, without oversight, and without transparency. The official charter on the topic was published only in November 2017 – more than half a year after the incident. Moreover, it is only an internal executive branch policy document but not a legal act, which means it may easily be changed or overlooked.

Secondly, there was no coordination mechanism to address cross-border cyber attacks. The worm affected 150 countries within several hours. The United States officially attributed the attack to North Korea seven months later. At this moment, however, the discussion on the issue was already over and any potential deterrent response failed.

What Happened

The Colonial Pipeline runs from Texas Gulf Coast to the New York Metropolitan area, carrying refined petroleum products (gasoline, jet fuel, diesel fuel, heating fuel) from various refineries. This pipeline has an annual throughput capacity of about 2.5 million barrels per day and serves the East Coast of the US with 45% of its refined fuel needs. On May 6, 2021, the ransomware group named DarkSide (a Russian-language affiliate program) gained access to Colonial Pipeline's information systems via one vulnerable employee's VPN account that had been abandoned previously, did not feature multi-factor authentication, and used credentials that had already been exposed in the past data breach.

Colonial Pipeline paid $4.4 million of the requested ransom in Bitcoins very soon after being targeted. In fact, the decryption tool provided by DarkSide was extremely inefficient, thus Colonial Pipeline had to restore systems from its backups. By May 12, Colonial Pipeline resumed its activities, though at a reduced capacity until returning to normal operations within a few days. On June 7, 2021, the FBI confirmed recovery of 63.7 Bitcoins worth about $2.3 million from one of the wallets associated with DarkSide. In fact, DarkSide ceased operations on May 14 under pressure from law enforcement and rebranded into BlackMatter within a couple of months, while some of them were eventually detected again operating as ALPHV/BlackCat (targeting Change Healthcare).

The Damage

Fuel shortage problems were prevalent in the southeast. Panic buying led to long queues at service stations from Virginia to Florida. Emergencies were declared in Virginia, North Carolina, Georgia, and Florida. Fuel prices in the US temporarily shot up past $3 per gallon, the first time since 2014. The airlines flying out of Charlotte Douglas and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta airports were forced to find new routes to refuel or bring additional fuel to their planes from the point of departure. In addition, the outage showed how vulnerable the downstream logistics system was; there weren’t enough tanker trucks available to make up for the loss.

What It Showed

The Colonial incident marked the point at which ransomware entered the realm of National Security Council priorities. As a consequence of the ransomware attack, President Biden signed executive order 14028 "Improving the Nation's Cybersecurity" (May 12, 2021), which included the following: a zero-trust architecture requirement for all federal government agencies; software supply chain security requirements, including the mandate for Software Bill of Materials to be supplied for all contracts with federal agencies; and creation of the Cyber Safety Review Board. In addition, two weeks after the shutdown, TSA issued its first-ever directive for the pipeline industry – Security Directive Pipeline-2021-01 – on May 28. Previously, the industry guidance had existed only in the form of voluntary TSA Pipeline Security Guidelines (2018).

Prior to 2021, TSA had not issued a single cybersecurity-related rule for the pipeline industry since its creation in 2001. Also, TSA had not conducted any cybersecurity-related audit of Colonial during the three years leading up to the cyberattack. One unsecured user account should not have caused almost 45% of the East Coast's fuel supplies to be shut down, yet it did.

We had no idea, at any of our facilities, that we had a vulnerability... In hindsight, we should have had multifactor authentication on [the VPN account], and we didn't. — Joseph Blount, CEO of Colonial Pipeline, testimony to US Senate Committee on Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs, June 8, 2021

What Happened

Change Healthcare is a subsidiary company under the Optum umbrella of UnitedHealth Group. It handles over 15 billion health care transactions per year, which include claims management, eligibility verification, prior authorisations, and prescription writing. As per estimates by the parent company, one in every three patient records in the United States passes through their systems at some point. UHG bought Change Healthcare for $13 billion in 2022.

An affiliate of the ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware gang gained access to a Change Healthcare Citrix remote access portal in February 2024. In an appearance before the Senate Finance Committee, on May 1, 2024, the CEO of UnitedHealth Group, Mr. Andrew Witty, stated that the portal lacked MFA. This was confirmed in his written statement and testified again during his appearance at the House Energy and Commerce Committee. Three years after the incident involving Colonial Pipeline, in one of the largest healthcare organisations in the United States, a similar failure led to a massive breach.

The hackers spent approximately nine days on the network between February 12 and February 21. They exfiltrated massive amounts of data during this period. They then launched ransomware against the company's core processing systems. Change Healthcare then disconnected all its services, which led to massive downstream effects. Patients could not verify their insurance coverage, submit claims online, or receive their medications unless they paid upfront in cash. Hospitals, dental practices, and physician groups could not file their claims and began to run out of operating capital in less than a month.

The Damage

The short-term impact on healthcare delivery was quite serious. In response to the incident, the American Hospital Association surveyed their members and discovered that 94% had experienced financial consequences, 74% reported disruptions to direct patient care, and more than half were experiencing payroll difficulties. Small healthcare providers, rural hospitals, and community health centers bore the brunt of the damage since they lacked the funds to survive without revenue for weeks. Consequently, HHS established an advance payment program for affected healthcare organisations.

UnitedHealth Group's direct costs resulting from the breach through fiscal year-end 2024 amounted to $3.09 billion. The estimate was taken from UnitedHealth Group's own Q1-Q4 2024 quarterly earnings reports. These costs cover information technology restoration efforts, business disruption expenses, and notification of customers. They do not account for litigation costs, including multiple class-action suits filed by patients, healthcare providers, and business partners.

I am deeply, deeply sorry to those individuals whose data has been compromised... Change Healthcare was in the process of upgrading the server that was compromised, and the fact that we had not yet deployed multi-factor authentication was an oversight for which there is no excuse. — Andrew Witty, CEO, UnitedHealth Group, testimony to US Senate Finance Committee, May 1, 2024

What It Showed

The first thing that became clear about the Change Healthcare attack is the issue of concentration risk within the US healthcare payment systems. No single firm should have the ability to bring down one-third of the country's healthcare billing due to its failure to properly protect a remote access portal with multi-factor authentication. However, due to decades of industry consolidation, Change Healthcare now finds itself at a critical junction – one which has an impact on nearly all Americans' experiences with the healthcare system in one way or another. Yet unlike in other industries where there are designations such as the SIFI framework, there is currently no such thing as a "systemically important" healthcare firm.

Second, the problem is that the HIPAA Security Rule itself is outdated. The most recent version of the rule's operative language was amended back in 2013. In that document, there is no requirement for multi-factor authentication, no requirement for any kind of encryption standard to be applied, no requirement for an inventory of assets, and no requirement for any sort of network segmentation. The only requirement regarding authentication is for covered entities to simply "implement procedures to verify that a person or entity seeking access to electronic protected health information is the one claimed."

Failures are diverse, but the process remains constant. There is a clear and avoidable weakness. There is no mandatory measure for its correction. The attack generates an outcome that is hugely disproportionate to the technical challenge of penetrating the network. Policy-making occurs belatedly, always reacting rather than anticipating, and never considering the implications beyond the immediately affected industry.

US Framework

A Patchwork of Authorities

Two decades of cybersecurity policy developed case-by-case with crucial vulnerabilities being repeatedly exposed by ransomware attackers. Most policies and guidelines are voluntary. The most relevant legislation currently in existence is yet to be fully implemented.

Timeline

How We Got Here

2003

HSPD-7 / National Strategy to Secure Cyberspace

Homeland Security Presidential Directive 7 was the first step made towards defining the critical infrastructures and determining federal responsibility regarding them. This strategy was very clear in terms of public private partnership and lacked any mandatory requirements or any means for enforcing them. It marked the way for the following twenty years.

2013

PPD-21 + EO 13636

PPD-21 re-designated the 16 CI Sectors and gave each an SRMA. EO 13636 then instructed NIST to develop a framework that became known as the Cybersecurity Framework. This is now used across the globe by businesses and governments alike. What's more, this remains completely voluntary for all but one of the 16 CI Sectors.

2015

Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act (CISA 2015)

Legal immunities were granted (liability immunities, antitrust immunities, FOIA immunities) to firms participating in voluntary programs that provide threat intelligence to the government. It was a great move, although few firms participated because of other liability issues as well as potential damage to their reputation.

2018

CISA Established

With the enactment of the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency Act of 2018, the National Protection and Programs Directorate under the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) evolved to become an independent agency known as the CISA. The organisation acts as the primary coordination body for federal civilian cybersecurity in the United States. However, the powers of CISA are advisory in nature regarding private organisations.

2021

Executive Order 14028

Issued immediately following SolarWinds and Colonial Pipeline attacks. Executive Order 14028 “Improving the Nation’s Cybersecurity” (May 12, 2021) called for the implementation of a zero trust model and EDR for federal agencies; implemented software supply chain security measures including SBOMs for federal contractors; imposed incident reporting for federal contractors; and established the Cyber Safety Review Board. Extensive coverage within federal government and its contractors. Minimal impact on private CI outside federal government scope.

2022

CIRCIA Signed

The Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act of 2022, signed March 15, 2022 as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act (Pub. L. 117-103). First federal law addressing cross-sector reporting in cyberspace. Mandates CISA to promulgate rules regarding 72-hour reporting for substantial cyber incidents and 24-hour reporting for ransom payments. Congress allowed CISA to define "covered entity" and "substantial cyber incident."

2023

National Cybersecurity Strategy

White House, National Cybersecurity Strategy (March 2023). This is the first strategy paper that recognises “shifting the burden” to software companies and service providers from end users. It also recommended mandatory cybersecurity requirements for critical infrastructure. This strategy paper set the direction for the HIPAA Security Rule NPRM and other sectoral regulations.

2024

NSM-22 + CIRCIA NPRM + HIPAA NPRM

Three key regulatory moves. National Security Memorandum 22, issued on April 30, 2024, enhanced the framework for critical infrastructure, effectively revising PPD-21. The proposed CIRCIA Rule by CISA covered about 316,000 entities for mandatory reporting requirements. Finally, on December 27, 2024, HHS/OCR released an NPRM for the HIPAA Security Rule, which is the first big revision in over two decades and which requires multifactor authentication, encryption, asset management, and network segmentation at all HIPAA entities.

2025-26

CIRCIA Delayed to May 2026

The final CIRCIA rule was intended to be released by CISA by October 2025. The comments were received in such huge numbers, even exceeding 2,000, that there was a need for the agency to extend the deadline to May 2026. Virtual town hall meetings were announced in February 2026, but a lapse in funding of DHS prevented these meetings from taking place. Even in April 2026, the release of the final CIRCIA rule remains pending.

CIRCIA Up Close

What CIRCIA Actually Requires

Because CIRCIA is the single most important piece of ransomware-relevant federal legislation in the last decade, it is worth walking through what it actually says versus what it will require once operational.

Who is covered?

"A covered entity" under the law means an entity within one of the 16 critical infrastructure sectors defined as such under CISA rules. The making of those rules is the essence of the battle. CISA's April 2024 NPRM sets forth a dual test whereby a covered entity is any entity in one of the critical infrastructure sectors, which either (1) surpasses SBA size thresholds based on its NAICS industry code classification, or (2) irrespective of size, qualifies under various criteria specific to each individual sector (chemical facilities with over a certain threshold amount of material present, defense industrial base companies, entities that own or operate bulk-power electric systems, and more). CISA estimates that about 316,000 entities will be covered under the new rulemaking.

Why the delay matters

The CIRCIA law was signed in March 2022 due to Colonial Pipeline, SolarWinds, and the need for federal visibility into cyber events. The law allocated 24 months for the NPRM to be published by CISA, followed by 18 months from the publication of the NPRM until the rule is issued. CISA complied with the NPRM deadline in April 2024 but moved the deadline for issuance of the final rule from October 2025 to May 2026. Even this timeline faced the possibility of being breached due to the lapse in the budget of DHS, which made the February 2026 town halls impossible to conduct. Each month's delay in the process means another month of blindness regarding ransomware reports. The Change Healthcare attack occurred two years after the start of the CIRCIA schedule.

The gap between urgency and implementation. CIRCIA was signed in direct response to Colonial Pipeline and SolarWinds, but the final rule has been pushed back repeatedly.

Analysis

The Five Gaps

1. Voluntary means optional

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework is a well-designed document. In fact, it is a framework by design. Thus, it outlines cybersecurity goals across its six functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) and then adds categories and subcategories below them. It does not tell anyone what to do.

The healthcare sector is subject to HIPAA Security Rule. This rule dates back to 2003 and has had a substantive update as recently as in 2013. It requires neither multi-factor authentication nor encryption standards or any such specifics. The single-sentence section regarding authentication is all there is in the HIPAA Security Rule. Furthermore, its enforcement by HHS is a backward look that assesses whether a breached organisation had "reasonable and appropriate" protection; many of those fined for breaches usually agree to pay relatively minor amounts to resolve disputes. The Change Healthcare breach happened in an environment where the HIPAA Security Rule technically required "authentication procedures," but nothing that could prevent this kind of attack.

Water/wastewater services are even more of a problem. There are about 150,000 water utility companies in the United States; the vast majority of those are small municipalities. The EPA tried to force its hand using the Safe Drinking Water Act, by requiring water utilities to conduct cybersecurity assessments in their risk assessments. However, in Attorneys General of Missouri et al. v. EPA (8th Cir., 2023), the Eight Circuit ruled in favor of the Attorneys General, finding that the statute did not give the EPA the authority to do that. As of early 2026, there is no cybersecurity requirement in water utilities.

2. Fragmentation across sixteen agencies

The 16 CI sectors are overseen by 16 separate SRMAs, although certain agencies regulate more than one sector, especially CISA, which regulates eight sectors. These organisations vary greatly in their powers, resources, culture, and historical performance. Some, such as the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and financial regulators, are safety-and-soundness professionals with long histories of enforcement of complicated technical standards. Others, like EPA with respect to water, possess very little cybersecurity know-how and legal authority.

3. CIRCIA is running years behind

CIRCIA became law in March 2022 as a result of the Colonial Pipeline and SolarWinds incidents demonstrating that there was no complete visibility for the government on cyber attacks at the critical infrastructure level. The deadlines set out under the legislation (24 months from introduction to NPRM, 18 months from NPRM to final rule) were reflective of this urgency. However, when the NPRM finally arrived in April 2024, it was highly controversial. It was claimed by industry organisations that the dual test of coverage was overly broad, that the 72-hour deadline to report was impractical due to ongoing incident response activities, that the record retention provisions were too strict, and that the ransomware payment reporting requirement incentivised non-cooperation.

4. Disruption without persistence

American law enforcement agencies and their intelligence services are not lacking in the technical capabilities to interfere with ransomware activities. The FBI's anti-Hive ("Dagon") operation was conducted between July 2022 and January 2023. In the seven-month duration of that operation, FBI officers remained within Hive's IT infrastructure collecting encryption keys in stealth mode and handing them out to their owners who were not ready to pay, helping more than 300 people to save $130 million. Finally, the FBI took down Hive's servers on January 26, 2023, together with its German and Dutch counterparts.

Disruption of ALPHV/BlackCat in December 2023 followed the same pattern. Through cooperation between the FBI and its foreign partners, ALPHV's leak site on the dark web was taken down, and the group's decryption tool was released. Weeks later, ALPHV had restored its infrastructure using other servers and got back to work. Within two months, an ALPHV affiliate was responsible for an attack on Change Healthcare. The problem is not that disruption cannot succeed; disruption obviously succeeds temporarily. Disruption will change nothing if it's limited to temporary operations. Disruption has to be sustained because these groups have no capital structure that can be destroyed and their infrastructure rebuilt within days with a new list of affiliates.

5. CISA cannot compel anyone in the private sector

However, that power ends with the federal networks. CISA cannot instruct a hospital to mitigate vulnerabilities on its KEV list. CISA cannot instruct a water utility to implement multi-factor authentication. CISA cannot require a pipeline company to install an endpoint detection and response system. CISA can advise, coordinate, collaborate, and provide free technical support. Whether or not the private actor implements those recommendations is solely up to them. That is the critical structural problem. The federal organisation with the greatest technical expertise and situational awareness of the threat lacks any power to ensure compliance. After the Colonial attack, TSA received new powers over pipelines. Following the Change Healthcare hack, HHS is working on updating HIPAA. Yet CISA, the interagency coordination organisation, remains voluntary.

Framework

NIST CSF 2.0

The second edition of the Cybersecurity Framework from NIST was released in February 2024. This version of the framework introduced changes in the core structure by adding a sixth function called Govern. The new function sits in the middle of the other five, which include Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. The introduction of Govern is seen as the biggest development since the first release of the framework ten years ago. The new version emphasizes that cybersecurity should be treated as an organisational challenge and not purely as a technical issue.

UK Comparison

What the UK Built Instead

Centralised administration, standards for vital services backed up by force, and defensive measures on a nationwide level. This is not the solution to all issues, nor can some aspects of it be exported into America directly. However, several elements from the UK system directly tackle the very deficiencies highlighted by the American case studies.

Institutional Design

The NCSC: One Agency, Not Seven

The United Kingdom established the National Cyber Security Centre in October of 2016 as the publicly facing operation component of GCHQ (Government Communications Headquarters), the UK equivalent of NSA. This is on purpose. The UK model is such that the agency responsible for carrying out offensive signals intelligence is also responsible for the defensive cybersecurity efforts of the country, working through the same operational chain of command, sharing analysts, the same infrastructure and sharing threat intelligence real-time without the need for any classification considerations. This would not go down well in the US because of the established split between signals intelligence and civilian cybersecurity in the US, as well as justifiably, civil liberty concerns. But it does mean something.

Alongside the integration of its intelligence, the NCSC is responsible for running the Active Cyber Defence (ACD) program. ACD comprises a series of automated, national-scale defensive measures that are part of public infrastructure. Protective DNS prevents the querying of malicious domains at scale for all public-sector entities in the UK. Takedown services remove phishing pages and malware from UK infrastructure (millions of malicious URLs have been taken down each year by the NCSC). Mail Check provides DMARC/SPF/DKIM-based email authentication at scale for government agencies and more. This makes spoofing UK Government emails difficult. The Suspicious Email Reporting Service (SERS) allows anyone in the public to report any phishing emails to a specific email address.

The NCSC is the UK's technical authority on cyber security. Since our launch in 2016, we have worked to make the UK the safest place to live and work online. Active Cyber Defence seeks to reduce the harm from commodity cyber attacks by providing tools and services that protect against a wide range of cyber security threats. — NCSC, "Active Cyber Defence: The Fourth Year," 2021
Mandatory Standards

The NIS Regulations and the CAF

It is here that the approach taken by the UK differs from the US most strongly. The Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/506) are the UK's version of the EU NIS Directive (2016/1148). OES are required to undertake "appropriate and proportionate technical and organisational measures to manage the risks posed to the security of network and information systems that they use in order to provide those essential services". That is a mandatory requirement; it is not voluntary.

Competent authorities are appointed to enforce regulations within each regulated sector. Ofgem does this for energy; Ofcom for digital infrastructure and telecommunications; the Department for Health and Social Care for the NHS, etc. Competent authorities can inspect sites, compel remedial action through issuance of an enforcement notice, and impose financial penalties of up to £17 million per violation for the most serious offences. Reporting of incidents is mandatory. The operator of the essential service must report to the competent authority within 72 hours of discovery of any incident affecting continuity of that service.

The problem of determining whether an OES satisfies the "appropriate and proportionate measures" test is solved using the NCSC's Cyber Assessment Framework (CAF). The current version of CAF is 3.2, and it is designed according to four overarching objectives, namely, (A) the management of security risks; (B) protection from cyber attacks; (C) detection of cyber security incidents; and (D) mitigation of the adverse effects of such incidents. For each of these objectives, there are 14 contributing principles, and underneath each principle, there are "contributing outcomes" and "indicators of good practice" (IGPs).

The approach underlying CAF is outcome-based and not prescriptive. The framework does not tell the regulated organisations that they have to deploy TLS 1.3 or that they have to use a certain EDR vendor. Instead, it states that "you protect data stored in your network and information systems from unauthorised access, modification, or deletion" and then goes on to describe what "achieved," "partially achieved," and "not achieved" mean. Each competent authority determines its sector's "CAF Profiles" in accordance with the desired level of accomplishment that regulated organisations need to attain. There are only two recognised profile levels of the CAF: Basic (sector baseline) and Enhanced (advanced threats).

That is the system that America lacks. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) explains what good looks like but does not include a required level. The Cyber Assessment Framework (CAF) explains what good looks like, sets a required level for each objective, and mandates that companies must achieve that level, with financial penalties for failure.

Takeaways

What Actually Translates

Outcome-based mandatory baselines are achievable. NIS Regulations provide proof that a government can mandate desired security results from ES providers without mandating technology or limiting innovation. CAF's objective of four is very close to how sector regulators in the United States consider cybersecurity issues. The HIPAA Security Rule NPRM, for example, seeks to achieve the same objectives in the healthcare industry. What the United Kingdom demonstrates is that this process can be generalised horizontally to cover all critical infrastructure sectors rather than litigating on a sector-by-sector basis each time there is an incident.

ACD in the UK is an example of how nation-wide automated defense systems can work, being both technically possible and effective. Every year, they protect the network from millions of attacks at nearly zero marginal cost per customer. The protective domain name system operated by CISA is technically identical, but works only for federal agencies and a handful of SLTTs and commercial customers who voluntarily participate. Scaling up such systems to cover the entire nation is possible and straightforward from an engineering perspective.

A single lead agency with statutory authority is faster than inter-agency coordination. The idea of one NCSC rather than the sum total of CISA, NSA, FBI, and sixteen SRMAs as seen in the British system is not easily reproducible. However, the key point to be taken from this is the possibility of empowering CISA's responsibilities to a much larger extent (as in Recommendation 3) without disbanding DHS, FBI, and NSA altogether. The relevant issue to discuss would thus not be whether CISA should turn into MI5/GCHQ, but how far should its scope extend vis-à-vis the private sector.

Recommendations

Six Things That Should Change

The recommendations are based on failures that are outlined in the case studies. These failures are then linked to gaps in the existing theory. The recommendations then propose changes that should be made. These do not involve developing any new technology. What is needed is a political will to utilise the available technology.

01

Mandatory Standards

Outcomes-based cyber baselines for all 16 CI sectors, modeled on the UK CAF.

02

Finish CIRCIA

Publish the final rule. Close the reporting gap. Actually collect the data.

03

Give CISA Teeth

Expand BOD authority to cover the private sector during active incidents.

04

Concentration Risk

SIFI-style designation for systemically important CI entities.

05

Active Defense

National-scale ACD plus sustained, continuous disruption operations.

06

Legislate the VEP

Statutory framework with independent oversight. Default to disclosure.

01

Mandatory Minimum Cybersecurity Standards for All 16 CI Sectors

What should change

Congress needs to create a sector-agnostic cybersecurity benchmark based on outcomes rather than specific technologies, similar to the UK's Cyber Assessment Framework. This benchmark must describe what the outcome of an organisation's cybersecurity practices should be without tying itself down to current technologies that will soon become obsolete.

At a minimum, the following must be required by this baseline: multi-factor authentication for remote access and privileged user access (phishing-resistant factors for highest-privilege users), remediation of all vulnerabilities listed in CISA's KEV catalog within the same time frames as BOD 22-01 requires for federal agencies, logical segregation of IT from OT environments and business environments from those handling regulated data, encryption of, testing of, and segregating of backups from their primary storage offline, incident response plan development and regular exercises, and some level of supply chain risk management and vendor security attestations.

Any discussion about whether cybersecurity standards should be made mandatory in critical infrastructures comes to an end with Colonial and Change Healthcare. The only thing that remains is the issue of designing these standards, meaning how to develop them in a way that ensures their enforceability but also leaves them non-prescriptive and proportional.

02

Finish CIRCIA Implementation

What the gap is

CIRCIA was designed precisely to address these shortcomings. Enacted in March 2022, the law obligated CISA to publish a final rule within 42 months (until September 2025). CISA met its NPRM deadline in April 2024, but moved its deadline for publishing a final rule back from October 2025 to May 2026. The planned February 2026 town hall meetings, intended to provide additional public feedback, were disrupted due to a funding lapse at DHS. In April 2026, a final rule had yet to be issued. Four years after the enactment, mandatory reporting was still not in place.

What should change

The CISA must work towards finalizing and issuing the rule before the deadline of May 2026 despite the possibility of having to limit the coverage initially only to expand it gradually via future rulemaking processes. An imprecise rule that is functional is much better than the precise rule that is delayed by another two years.

In particular, CISA must: (1) adopt a tiered process whereby the biggest companies in each industry are initially required to report, followed by the smaller companies as the intake capability of CISA is expanded; (2) define "reasonable belief" concerning the 72-hour reporting requirement such that there would be reasonable justification for reporting delay when the scope of the event remains unclear; (3) finalise the definition of a substantial cyber incident based on practical examples (shutting down a pipeline, shutting down a payment processor, shutting down a hospital); and (4) establish the legal protection provided by effectively to encourage compliance.

03

Give CISA Real Authority for Active Incidents

What the gap is

The statutory jurisdiction of CISA grants it wide-ranging coordination, information sharing, and technical assistance capabilities. The Binding Operational Directive jurisdiction granted by the Cybersecurity Act of 2015 only applies to the federal civilian executive branch agencies. In the case of the private sector, CISA has limited jurisdiction in terms of advisory guidance, alerts, and the Joint Cyber Defense Collaborative (JCDC), which is a voluntary information sharing arrangement.

What should change

The Congress should modify the CISA's statutory authorities to establish an "Emergency Cyber Security Directive" that will allow CISA to compel owners and operators of critical infrastructure during an ongoing incident. The new power must target the specific problem highlighted by the two case studies – that is, a known weakness that is under attack while the other targets remain vulnerable.

This is not about making CISA a general cybersecurity regulator. Rather, this is about enabling CISA with the appropriate authority to compel hospitals across the nation to shut down unmaintained Citrix portals during the three-week window following the Change Healthcare hack, while the exact tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) were actively employed against other healthcare organisations. This would have represented an important contribution to national security, with virtually no impact on organisations not affected by the threat.

04

Address Concentration Risk: Systemically Important CI Entities

What the gap is

The regulation for the security in America such as the HIPAA Security Rule NPRM focuses on entities rather than systems. In America, the regulation for a 500-bed hospital is very similar to that of the entity processing a third of the country's health care transaction system. The only parallel that comes close to the designation of SIFI in CI would be the creation of this type of designation in the banking sector after the 2008 financial crisis. The creation of the Systematically Important Financial Institutions through the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 enables the Financial Stability Oversight Council to designate non-bank financial institutions which pose risks of failure to the financial system as SIFIs.

What should change

It is recommended that Congress establish a similar designation process for organisations providing digital infrastructure services, the disruption of which may endanger a critical infrastructure sector. The designating entity would be an already established one (such as CISA itself, or a newly formed interagency council with CISA and the appropriate SRMA co-chairing), based on considerations such as market concentration, interconnectedness, substitutability, complexity, and multi-sector operations.

05

Build Active Defense at Scale and Sustain Disruption

What the gap is

The US has two separate capabilities that can be described as being grossly under-utilised considering the severity of the threat. First of all, it concerns the implementation of active defense against cyberattacks at the national level via automatic prevention of malware traffic, DNS filtering, email authentication, and elimination of malicious infrastructure. While CISA's Protective DNS is present, it is only utilised by the federal government and some SLTT and private entities. At the same time, NCSC of the UK has such capabilities at a true national scale.

What should change

Protective DNS, along with other cyber defensive services, should be scaled up to national-level implementation. Congress needs to mandate funding for CISA to deploy Protective DNS to every state, local, territorial, and tribal (SLTT) government by default, all hospitals that qualify for Medicare or Medicaid payment, all water utilities serving more than a certain customer base, and any organisation opting into Protective DNS. An email-checking service similar to NCSC's Mail Check needs to be deployed, mandating DMARC, SPF, and DKIM enforcement on any domain used by a federally-regulated entity.

06

Legislate the Vulnerabilities Equities Process

What the gap is

The Vulnerability Equities Policy is the internal process through which the US government determines how to handle the decision-making process regarding whether any new discovery of vulnerabilities should either be disclosed to the vendor or withheld for offensive uses. The VEP Charter was released in November 2017, half a year following the WannaCry incident, officially adopting the policy as executive policy. The process features an Equities Review Board, headed by an official at the National Security Council level, comprised of various agency representatives, including NSA, CIA, FBI, DHS, State, Treasury, Commerce, DOD, DOE, ODNI, and OMB.

While it is a step in the right direction, and an improvement from how things were before 2017, it suffers from three major flaws. First of all, it is only policy, not law. This means that it could be changed at the will of whatever executive administration is in power. Secondly, there is no requirement under statute for any form of independent oversight. The congressional intelligence committees receive some reports, but there is nothing similar to a FISA Court to review these decisions. Finally, the true criteria used for these decisions, as well as their rates of retention and disclosure, remain classified.

What should change

VEP process should be codified into law via amending the National Security Act of 1947 or its equivalent. Critical considerations would be: (1) statutory default in favor of disclosure where retention is an exception; (2) enumerated criteria that need to be taken into account, such as extent of vulnerability in terms of critical US infrastructure, potential harm to the nation and its citizens from discovery by adversaries (as demonstrated by the WannaCry attacks), likely lifespan until independent discovery of the vulnerability and whether there are other offensive options available; (3) oversight body, which may take the form of an inspector general with cybersecurity expertise or a dedicated sub-committee for intelligence oversight within the Congress; (4) mandated disclosure to affected vendors based on set timelines if retention criteria have not been satisfied; (5) periodic public reporting of aggregate data (numbers of vulnerabilities examined, number released to vendors, average turnaround for disclosure).

It should be noted that ransomware is not a natural phenomenon. Ransomware is a consequence of deliberate choices made through policy, which has led to the creation of an ecosystem where ransomware attacks are inexpensive to conduct, expensive to endure, and seldom penalised. Each recommendation presented in this report relates to some deficiency described in the case study analysis. The means are available.

Counterarguments

The best case against these proposals

A policy proposal has to answer the strongest version of the case against it, not the easiest one. What follows engages the five objections that will actually be raised in hearings, in industry briefs, and in the academic literature. The structure for each is the same: the strongest form of the argument first, then the response.

01

Checkbox Compliance

Mandatory standards drift into paperwork exercises that do not improve security.

02

Federalism Limits

Emergency CISA directives over private industry are legally exposed and politically unworkable.

03

SIFI Ossification

Cybersecurity designation entrenches dominant firms rather than addressing concentration.

04

Intel Equities

Codifying VEP disclosure degrades US offensive cyber at a moment of great-power competition.

05

Execution Capacity

CISA cannot deliver on what it already has; adding mandates compounds failure.

Objection 01

Mandatory standards produce checkbox compliance, not security

The argument

The primary objection to Recommendation 1 lies in the fact that mandatory outcome-based requirements gradually turn into a list of requirements to be checked. Auditors need evidence, and companies will provide evidence; an item such as “Ensure protection of information against any unauthorized access” turns into a documented system controlled by third-party specialists interested in extending the system. The second argument against the recommendation is even more powerful: The HIPAA Security Rule has existed for 20 years, and in 2024, Change Healthcare failed because of not implementing MFA.

The response

However, the HIPAA example actually cuts the other way. The Security Rule did not undergo any revisions after the HITECH Omnibus in 2013. The rule does not explicitly require multi-factor authentication, encryption in transit, or inventorying of assets. Moreover, the rule's authentication requirements are so general that even a simple password meets them. The NPRM for updating the HIPAA Security Rule released in December 2024 proves that the existing rule did not impose sufficiently stringent standards. However, while a mandatory regulation can be made stricter through rulemaking and enforcement, a voluntary standard cannot since there will be no compliance notices for violations issued by NIST to a local hospital. This trade-off is addressed in the UK's CAF profiles – the Basic one and Enhanced. They have been prioritized based on their criticality and thus, a 200-bed rural hospital would need a completely different profile compared to that of a national payments processor.

The correct read of the HIPAA record is not that mandatory standards do not work. It is that standards without teeth, and without revision, produce exactly the failure mode one would predict.

Objection 02

Emergency CISA authority over the private sector is legally and politically unworkable

The argument

The third recommendation would allow for a civilian entity to compel private businesses to take action in the midst of an ongoing situation, thus providing federal authority with additional jurisdiction over intrastate commerce. Every order would be subject to judicial scrutiny under the Administrative Procedure Act on grounds of failure to provide proper notice and opportunity for comment, and every compelled shutdown would be subject to claims of regulatory taking under the Fifth Amendment. This recommendation has not been stress-tested against two decades of congressional inaction on this precise matter.

The response

The narrower tailoring lowers legal risks. The new authority will be invoked only when there is evidence of a known vulnerability being actively exploited by a threat actor against CI peers, and there is also a solution at hand. All three criteria fall under the good cause exemption to APA. Good cause has been successfully applied before, as was done by TSA Security Directive Pipeline-2021-01 published without notice-and-comment process in May 2021. No challenge has succeeded in overturning the directive. A new CISA authority based on the logic above is just an extension of an existing legal tool used in transportation security to an incrementally different context. On political feasibility, Congress had acted promptly after Colonial Pipeline, introducing the CIRCIA in less than 10 months. However, what stopped other similar bills from passing is not lack of political will but diffused accountability among the sixteen SRMAs, each one having a neighboring authority that can step up to respond.

Objection 03

SIFI-style designation ossifies market structure and creates moral hazard

The argument

The designation of Change Healthcare as a “systemically important” firm stands in opposition to the use of antitrust remedies as an alternative to dealing with concentrated industries. This approach does not make much sense in terms of financial SIFIs: a federal court sided with MetLife in March 2016, the Financial Stability Oversight Council rescinded the designations of AIG in 2017 and Prudential in 2018, and many argue that the very act of designating them as systemically important led to their receiving a “too-big-to-fail” status, thus reducing the costs of capital for these companies and reinforcing their power.

The response

Cybersecurity designation is not financial SIFI designation. It does not increase expectations of bailouts since there is nothing to be bailed out; a defunct payments processing company would declare Chapter 11 bankruptcy and not obtain a liquidity loan from the federal government. Rather, it imposes more stringent cyber security requirements, necessitates dependency maps for all third-party services, and mandates reporting for organizations whose collapse would impact an entire industry. However, antitrust is the correct long-term remedy for addressing systemic risk from concentration, but it is lengthy, taking around a decade to complete. UnitedHealth Group completed its acquisition of Change Healthcare in October 2022, and the data breach occurred in February 2024. One cannot allow the lengthy process of antitrust to address concentration while cyber criminals exploit the vulnerability created by concentration in the interim. The MetLife case, once properly understood, was never an argument against designation. Judge Collyer's decision analyzed the FSOC's failure to adhere to its own guidance, as well as its unwillingness to consider the costs of designation, not the concept of designation itself. Thus, a cybersecurity regime that includes objective criteria (market share, substitutability, multi-sectoral integration, and third-party dependence among critical infrastructure organizations) and allows judicial review addresses this concern.

Objection 04

Codifying the VEP degrades US offensive cyber capability

The argument

Recommendation 6 calls for the creation of legislation that would automatically reveal any discovered vulnerabilities under the Vulnerabilities Equities Process. The NSA needs information on vulnerabilities that have yet to be patched in order to carry out cyber operations on state systems of China, Russia, North Korea, and Iran. A legally mandated automatic disclosure system with clear procedures as to what should be classified, and when vendors must be informed, will bring the decision-making process out from the secrecy of the executive branch into the light. It will also serve as a signal of future offensive capabilities. Other states such as Russia, China, North Korea, and Iran do not share the same constraints. The consequence is a one-sided decrease in US signals intelligence in the great power rivalry.

The response

This recommendation is not asking that all vulnerabilities be exposed. It is a legal construct where retaining a vulnerability is a permitted exception, not the rule. The criteria must be constructed in such a way as to preserve offensive capabilities: products from non-US vendors, no tangible presence in US critical infrastructure, a credible low-rediscovery risk assessment, and demonstrable operational utility. In this context, it would be permissible to retain a vulnerability in a Chinese industrial control system that is not present in any US critical infrastructure but not one in Windows SMB used in the WannaCry ransomware attack. WannaCry is the argument for acting here, not a reason to hesitate. EternalBlue was retained by the NSA for close to five years before its theft from the agency during the Shadow Brokers release in April 2017 and its use by North Korea in a ransomware attack against the National Health Service, Merck, FedEx, and Maersk just weeks later. The government will want to retain the vulnerabilities since the immediate operational utility is obvious while the cost is speculative. The legal construct must involve an independent oversight mechanism, a classified annex detailing operational considerations, and periodic public reporting on the aggregate results.

Objection 05

CISA cannot execute on what it already has

The argument

The primary concern with the package is that CISA is not capable of enforcing the requirements imposed upon it by Congress. Under CIRCIA, Congress gave the agency 42 months to issue a final rule. CISA met the NPRM deadline in April 2024 but has missed the final-rule deadline, which was pushed from October 2025 to May 2026, and its implementation was hindered by a budget shortfall. Four years have elapsed since the passage of CIRCIA without mandatory incident reporting being operationalized. Recommendation 2, 3, and 5 all assume that CISA is capable of enforcement and executing its emergency directive and ACD nationally.

The response

The above claim is closest to being factual and is constructive in nature. There are four solutions. Firstly, CISA should be provided with several years of appropriations as opposed to annual appropriations at discretion; CIRCIA's failure to hold workshops in February 2026 was due to the Continuing Resolution shortfall, which should not be able to hinder any rule making that is obligatory. Secondly, CIRCIA should begin operations by concentrating on the larger covered entities with a clearly defined strategy for involving the rest of the 316,000 entities under cover as recommended in Recommendation 2. Thirdly, if deadlines are not adhered to, there should be automatic provisions of defaults like those in Dodd-Frank; whenever an agency does not comply with its deadlines, minimum coverage provisions should kick in instead of continued negotiations with the regulated parties. Finally, the National-scale Active Cyber Defence (ACD) is nothing extraordinary from an engineering perspective because the NCSC developed Protective DNS in 2017, which came a year after the NCSC was established in October 2016, handling over 2.5 trillion DNS queries thus far.

What Remains

What Is Left Standing After the Objections

Collectively, the five objections strengthen, but do not refute, the recommendations. The checklist compliance argument supports Recommendation 1: an approach based on graduated standards, with proportionality already incorporated. The federalism argument calls for narrowly tailored regulations, sunsets, and retained judicial review: attributes that could be incorporated into Recommendation 3 and would mirror existing TSA authority. The SIFI objection calls for clear criteria for designation, along with reversibility, which strengthens Recommendation 4. The intelligence equities argument calls for carefully constructed exceptions in a statutory framework, which Recommendation 6 anticipates. The execution capacity objection calls for multi-year funding and narrow initial rule-making for CISA, as well as automatic defaults: enhancements that must come with the package.

But none of the objections, taken separately or together, provide a plausible basis for rejecting the findings that animate this report: the same low-cost, widely recognized measures have never been implemented, and the case studies described here have inevitably resulted, and future incidents will likewise result, unless the regulatory regime changes. A critical response may well point to aspects of the proposed solution that need further refinement. But no serious critic could reasonably suggest that the current situation represents a sound basis for regulation.

The strongest objections are not reasons to do nothing. They are instructions for how to design the intervention correctly.
