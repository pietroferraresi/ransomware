COMPSCI 255 // Cyber Policy

Ransomware Attacks on Critical Infrastructure

Policy Failures and a Path Forward

Pietro Ferraresi // 2026

Introduction

The Problem

In the early 2010s, the nature of ransomware was relatively simple. It encrypted files, asked for hundreds of dollars in bitcoin as payment, and left. This is no longer the case. Modern ransomware operations function similarly to organized crime on an enterprise level. Ransomware criminals run affiliate programs, provide victims with help desks, and employ professional negotiators. Furthermore, the target selection of such groups is based on entities which simply cannot afford downtime like hospitals treating patients, pipelines transporting fuel to a large number of consumers, and payment processors which process a majority of the nation's health care transactions.

None of this happens by chance. Modern ransomware criminal enterprises are deliberate and precise in their choice of victim organizations. They pursue targets where the cost of downtime is high enough to warrant paying millions of dollars as ransom. Such criminal operations usually reside in other countries, namely Russia, where they can safely conduct their operations as long as they do not target entities within their own jurisdictions.

According to the US government, there are sixteen critical infrastructure sectors which are crucial to the national security and the wellbeing of its citizens. Unfortunately, after two decades of cybersecurity policy initiatives, the framework for protecting such sectors is severely flawed. Protection responsibilities are scattered among many different agencies. Most requirements are purely voluntary. Where there are mandatory rules, there is little enforcement of them.

How ransomware-as-a-service works.

This site looks at how we got here. Three case studies (WannaCry, Colonial Pipeline, and Change Healthcare) show the same failures playing out again and again across seven years. From there, it breaks down the current U.S. policy framework and its gaps, compares it to what the UK has built, and ends with six concrete recommendations for what should change.

Why does the U.S. keep getting hit by ransomware attacks on critical infrastructure, and what actually needs to change?

Background

The 16 Critical Infrastructure Sectors

Presidential Policy Directive 21 (2013) identifies sixteen sectors and assigns each a lead federal agency called a Sector Risk Management Agency (SRMA). In practice, the security requirements across these sectors range from genuinely rigorous (financial services, nuclear) to essentially nonexistent (water systems, many healthcare providers).

Vulnerability

Why Critical Infrastructure Keeps Getting Hit

Can't Afford Downtime

A hospital’s emergency room does not have the ability to shut down its operations for a week while undergoing an overhaul of its computer networks. In the same vein, a pipeline that provides fuel to half the population on the East Coast cannot stop operating while an assessment is being made of its control systems for any weaknesses that might be exploited. Ransomware operators are quite well versed with such relationships and take advantage of them.

Ancient Systems, New Connections

There is a significant number of critical infrastructure components that function based on technology which was originally created without consideration of its use in networking. Reliability, as opposed to security, was a key requirement for development of industrial control systems, SCADA systems, and medical equipment. Within the last couple of decades, these devices have become increasingly integrated into company networks in order to enhance their functionality. Through this integration, hackers have found themselves able to access the process-control systems via common points like a compromised email account.

Safe Havens

The leading ransomware groups typically operate out of Russia and nearby areas, where law enforcement is less likely to intervene unless the group is attacking local organizations. The use of the RaaS model makes the problem even worse by making the process of creating, operating, and brokering access codes through which data is stolen more fragmented and therefore more difficult to attribute.

Nobody Knows the Full Picture

Organizations that suffer attacks will have significant motives for concealing this information. Disclosures could result in falling stock prices, lawsuits, more stringent regulatory oversight, and even loss of reputation. Until such mandated disclosures become reality as envisioned by CIRCIA (which had not been done as of early 2026), the government remains blind to the occurrence or extent of such cyberattacks.

What Happened

WannaCry is the name of a ransomware worm that exploits a vulnerability in Windows referred to as EternalBlue. From the perspective of policy, the EternalBlue vulnerability was found by the National Security Agency (NSA) but decided to hold it secretly to use it offensively against potential enemies rather than reporting to Microsoft to develop a patch to fix the flaw. The exploitation of the vulnerability became possible when the Shadow Brokers published it in April 2017. A patch (MS17-010) was issued by Microsoft in March 2017 two months before the attack; nonetheless, many systems were still vulnerable due to various reasons like the installation of legacy Windows XP operating system on hospitals and industries.

The worm spread automatically without any intervention from the user. Once it infected one computer, it would continue searching for other computers vulnerable to the attack and propagate. A security analyst, Marcus Hutchins, accidentally found out about the kill switch in the code of the virus when he registered the domain used in the malware code.

The Damage

The biggest consequences of this event occurred in the National Health Service (NHS) of the United Kingdom. More than 80 trusts of NHS hospitals were affected, alongside 595 GP surgeries. There were also 19,000 canceled appointments, which included those related to cancer treatment. Additionally, ambulances were rerouted, and some hospitals stopped receiving patients. The National Audit Office later found that several warnings about the risks related to cybersecurity of the NHS had been issued before the occurrence of WannaCry attack and none of those concerns had been adequately addressed.

In the USA, only a small number of hospitals was disrupted, like Heritage Valley Health System in Pennsylvania, but the bigger problem was associated with the source of the exploit. It is believed that the NSA knew about the exploitability of the bug, and did not reveal it until long enough for the code to become widespread. As soon as it happened, NSA lost its exclusive weapon.

What It Showed

Three main issues are discussed. Firstly, the Vulnerabilities Equities Process, the system in which the government decides whether to share or keep information about the vulnerability, was not working properly. Secondly, the process of fixing critical vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure, especially in the healthcare industry, was inadequate. Lastly, there was no way to coordinate efforts between nations to react promptly to a ransomware attack on multiple countries at once.

What Happened

The DarkSide group, based out of Eastern Europe and offering its service using the ransomware as a service approach, gained access to the network of Colonial Pipeline via a sole stolen credential for the use of the virtual private network (VPN) and one that lacked multi-factor authentication (MFA). The intrusion continued after gaining the access as the cybercriminals moved through the Colonial network, encrypting the billing and support business systems.

Important to note here is the fact that DarkSide never tried to disrupt the pipeline control systems, meaning it went after IT systems rather than operational technology (OT). However, Colonial was unable to ascertain whether any of the OT components were impacted. In any case, the operations could not be carried out by Colonial since it required the use of the billing systems to gauge how much fuel was being transported.

The Damage

Shortages of fuel were rampant within the southeastern part of the United States with gas stations running out of fuel, and consumers' buying behavior was marked by panic. This led to volatile prices, which saw price hikes and the declaring of emergencies in several states. Additionally, there were shortages of jet fuel affecting airports such as Charlotte and Atlanta. Colonial Pipeline lost $4.4 million which had to be paid to them in Bitcoins. The FBI managed to retrieve about $2.3 million from the total losses through the seizure of DarkSide's cryptocurrency wallets – a rare feat for the FBI. After this chain of events, DarkSide declared themselves bankrupt, but some members formed other groups like BlackMatter and ALPHV/BlackCat.

What It Showed

This event made ransomware part of the White House’s national security considerations. In the following weeks, President Biden signed Executive Order 14028 and TSA issued its first-ever binding directive on pipeline cybersecurity. But the real significance of the event is that there was no previous regulation on pipeline cybersecurity before the incident. Furthermore, Colonial Pipeline Company had not received any security checks for three consecutive years. It appears that IT-OT convergence—a situation where the malfunction of IT systems also affects physical operations—is widespread throughout most critical infrastructure sectors.

What Happened

Change Healthcare, which is owned by UnitedHealth Group, processes about one-third of all healthcare transactions in America, including insurance claims, billing, and prior authorizations. The ransomware BlackCat/ALPHV affiliate hacked the network through a Citrix remote access portal without multi-factor authentication, a similar breach as the Colonial Pipeline incident that occurred three years ago.

For nine days, the attackers stayed inside the network to steal data before deploying ransomware on February 21, 2024. On March 3, UnitedHealth transferred around $22 million worth of Bitcoin. Afterward, more difficulties arose; the leadership of ALPHV/BlackCat allegedly used up the money and refused to pay out the affiliate’s portion. In retaliation, the affiliate announced that the stolen information would be published through an organization called RansomHub.

The Damage

The pharmacies throughout the nation were not able to submit any claims to the insurance companies, which led to delays in compensating the hospitals for the services provided to patients. The small clinics, community centers, and rural hospitals were hit hardest since they do not have the necessary funds to sustain themselves without income for weeks. A whopping 192.7 million people had their private health details compromised, making this the largest breach of medical data recorded in American history.

What It Showed

This problem revolves around concentration risk. Change Healthcare is heavily embedded within the payment systems of the U.S. healthcare industry, and when it was affected, there was a systemic failure in the sector. Regulatory measures available currently are insufficient to cover this kind of systemic dependency. The HIPAA Security Rule has not been reviewed since 2013, and it fails to include any requirement for MFA or technical measures of this type. Additionally, the recent ALPHV affiliate attack shows that the RaaS model is prone to certain vulnerabilities.

U.S. Framework

A Patchwork of Authorities

Twenty years of cybersecurity policy, built crisis by crisis, with critical gaps that ransomware operators keep exploiting.

How We Got Here

2003

HSPD-7

First national cybersecurity strategy. Identified CI protection as a priority but gave nobody the authority to actually require anything. Voluntary partnerships only.

2013

PPD-21 + EO 13636

Designated the 16 CI sectors and told NIST to build a Cybersecurity Framework. NIST CSF became the most widely used cybersecurity reference standard in the world, but it was always voluntary.

2015

Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act

Created legal protections for companies that share threat data with the government. Good idea in theory, but voluntary participation meant uptake was limited.

2018

CISA Created

DHS stood up a dedicated cybersecurity agency. CISA became the main coordinating body for civilian cybersecurity, but its powers are advisory. It can warn, help, and coordinate. It cannot compel.

2021

EO 14028

The Colonial Pipeline response. Zero-trust mandates, software supply chain requirements, incident reporting. Significant but mostly applies to federal systems and government contractors, not private CI broadly.

2022

CIRCIA

First mandatory reporting law: 72 hours for incidents, 24 hours for ransom payments. But the details got punted to rulemaking, and that rulemaking has been delayed repeatedly.

2025-26

CIRCIA Still Not Done

Final rule pushed to May 2026. CISA is running town halls in early 2026 to gather more industry input. A DHS funding lapse disrupted even those. Four years after the law passed, it still is not operational.

Analysis

The Five Gaps

1. Voluntary means optional

The CSF offered by the NIST is a useful framework, although following it is not compulsory in many industries. In finance, mandatory standards are provided through the GLBA. The nuclear industry follows NRC regulations. On the other hand, for health care, there is HIPAA, but since its last update in 2013, there have been no substantial changes, and unlike multi-factor authentication, it does not require anything as basic as that. In the water sector, which includes over 150,000 public utilities (with most being small), there are practically no cybersecurity requirements enforced at the federal level.

2. Fragmentation across agencies

The 16 industries and the 16 lead agencies demonstrate highly varying levels of maturity and regulation. The TSA is now empowered to force cybersecurity on pipelines, following the Colonial Pipeline breach, while the EPA lacks a mandate to require the same for water utilities, and the HHS cannot order hospitals to enforce multi-factor authentication. Therefore, the most vulnerable industry dictates the national standard because adversaries will always choose the easiest target.

3. CIRCIA is years late

CIRCIA marked a definite improvement in its own right. Nevertheless, a four-year gap before regulatory action is too long a time in light of the dynamic and dangerous threat environment. The NPRM has come under criticism for including well over 300,000 organizations, and the regulation was postponed from October 2025 to May 2026. Furthermore, a lack of funds for the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has prevented any further efforts towards industry outreach. Meanwhile, there remains no clear picture of how often critical infrastructure experiences effects.

4. Disruption without persistence

The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) executed a seven-month long penetration operation against the Hive ransomware gang before the group’s shutdown in January 2023, offering decryption keys to over 300 victims during that period. This feat is certainly commendable. But in December 2023, the law enforcement agencies made a second successful move by seizing the ALPHV/BlackCat group’s infrastructure. Yet, in just a few weeks, ALPHV bounced back and attacked Change Healthcare in two months’ time. The technical expertise is clear to see. What is missing here is persistent pressure to change their economics.

5. CISA cannot compel anyone

The work carried out by CISA is excellent, as seen from the KEV list, the Protect DNS service, and advisories provided, among others, which have earned wide acclaim. Nevertheless, where CISA detects a vulnerability in use in the health industry, it issues a warning but does not have the legal mandate to force affected parties to install patches for the detected vulnerabilities.

Framework

NIST CSF 2.0

Updated in February 2024, the framework organizes cybersecurity into six functions. Version 2.0 added Govern at the center to emphasize that leadership and risk management are foundational.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 core functions. Source: NIST.gov

The framework is well-built and widely used. In the case of critical infrastructure, though, the very qualities that make it useful cause it to fail: the organization can claim that it works within the guidelines of the NIST CSF but at the same time runs unpatched systems, fails to implement multi-factor authentication for remote login, and never tests the backups it has in place.

UK Comparison

What the UK Built Instead

A centralized agency, mandatory standards, and active defense at national scale. Not perfect, but it addresses the specific gaps that keep showing up in the U.S. case studies.

The NCSC

One Agency, Not Seven

The UK created the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) in 2016 to serve as an outreach arm of the country's signals intelligence agency, GCHQ. This is important because the primary body responsible for cybersecurity within the country has ready access to intelligence while being the main point of contact between the government and companies, hospitals, and the general population concerning defensive cybersecurity issues.

Active Cyber Defence is an initiative of the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) under which autonomous blocking of identified malicious domains, removal of phishing websites based on UK infrastructure, and the enforcement of standards for email authentication take place on a nationwide level. It acts as a tool, rather than merely providing advice, which will be used across UK networks to provide an initial defence that is beyond what any single entity would need to create.

On the contrary, in the US, all these duties are distributed amongst different agencies, namely CISA, NSA, FBI, the Secret Service, and several sector-specific regulators, each with their own sets of authorities, budgets, classification levels, and other organizational differences.

U.S. vs. UK institutional structure.
Standards

NIS Regulations + the CAF

In the United Kingdom, there are NIS Regulations (Network and Information Systems) that were enacted in 2018 but updated in 2022, and which mandate operators of critical services within the energy, transport, health, water, and digital sectors to meet cybersecurity regulations. These regulators can investigate, issue enforcement notices, and even impose fines of up to £17 million.

The tool used for assessing organizations' compliance is CAF, or Cyber Assessment Framework, which was developed by the NCSC. The CAF uses an outcome-oriented approach; thus, it does not require specific software acquisitions but rather focuses on meeting security goals.

Takeaways

What Actually Translates

However, the UK model will not work in the US. The two countries’ jurisdictions vary in terms of size, regulatory environment, and the civil liberties issues resulting from a civilian cybersecurity agency housed inside a signals intelligence service, which does not perfectly transfer over to the American setting. Nevertheless, the following aspects specifically address the threats mentioned in the case studies:

Mandatory baselines are doable. The example set by the NIS Regulations proves that standards for critical infrastructure can be legally enforced without hindering innovation. The CAF uses an outcome-based system, which could be adapted by US sectors operating in a voluntary regime.

Active defense at scale works. The ACD stops millions of malicious emails and removes hundreds of thousands of phishing pages each year. Although the United States has the necessary capabilities to implement such a strategy, it has never been applied on that scale.

One authority is faster than seven. Since the entire function is contained within one agency, any threats are dealt with immediately. If something similar were to happen in the United States, CISA, NSA, FBI, and the corresponding sector’s regulator would need to coordinate their actions.

Recommendations

Six Things That Should Change

Each one is grounded in the case studies and the UK comparison. None of them require inventing new technology. They require political will.

01

Mandatory Minimum Standards for Every CI Sector

Congress needs to make sure that all sixteen sectors embrace compulsory cybersecurity baselines which are outcome-based and similar to the Cyber Assessment Framework (CAF) employed by the United Kingdom. The baseline criteria need to comprise at least the following: multi-factor authentication (MFA) for remote access; timely fixing of any vulnerabilities included in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list within the allocated time frame; network segmentation of IT and OT networks; regular backup encryption and testing; and annual exercising of the incident response plan.

The responsible authorities need to have sufficient authority as well as funding in order to check whether the companies comply with the established cybersecurity measures. There are gaps in current regulations: while the TSA is authorized to mandate cybersecurity in pipelines, neither the EPA can force the water sector firms to embrace MFA nor the HHS can compel hospitals to do the same. When it comes to smaller organizations facing resource shortages, their cybersecurity baselines should go together with federal grants and technical assistance. However, the baseline itself cannot be optional.

Mandatory standards being appropriate for critical infrastructure is no longer arguable based on the examples discussed. Rather, the real question revolves around how to create these standards while balancing issues of proportionality, enforcement, and adaptability in sixteen different sectors.

02

Finish CIRCIA

This four-year gap from passage of the legislation to its proper implementation does not hold ground owing to the continuous exacerbation of the issue that the law seeks to address. The rulemaking process should culminate with clear definitions, proper coverage, and strong legal protections in place to ensure that organizations provide truthful reports and do not see compliance as simply a legal obligation but as an effort towards preventing attacks through ransomware. Information reported by the firms should include any ransom payments made as part of the attacks.

03

Give CISA Real Authority

CISA must have the power to give binding operational orders to critical infrastructure operators during an ongoing incident, similar to how it can issue binding orders for federal agencies. In case there is a vulnerability being exploited by an adversary across the healthcare sector, CISA must have the capability to enforce patching and not just recommend it. This is not about turning CISA into a regular regulatory body for all industries; it’s simply about giving it the power to refer to other agencies within a particular industry sector.

04

Designate Systemically Important CI Entities

Change Healthcare was responsible for about one-third of all healthcare transactions in the United States, and Colonial Pipeline carried 45 percent of fuel on the East Coast. The collapse of such infrastructures may cause a domino effect that could impact an entire industry. The current laws are indifferent to the size of the company and do not consider the concentration risk factor. There needs to be a way that will be similar to the SIFI designation after the financial crisis of 2008, which will recognize companies that, when failing, will have a ripple effect on the industry.

05

Build Active Defense at Scale

However, automated tools are used by the United Kingdom's Anti-Cybercrime Domain program (ACD) for blocking malicious websites, phishing sites, and deploying email authentication across the country. Though the United States can develop the technical capability to employ similar actions, the current attempts by organizations like CISA's Protective DNS are mere first steps compared to the extent of accomplishment witnessed in the UK. In other words, the ability needs to be expanded. On the other hand, disruptive actions must continue without pause. For instance, the FBI's Hive action that lasted for seven months and helped more than 300 victims was very effective. It would be prudent to allocate funding to the Joint Ransomware Task Force on a perpetual basis.

06

Legislate the VEP

WannaCry was primarily driven by the NSA's choice to exploit a discovered vulnerability as a weapon rather than to reveal it to Microsoft for fixing. The Vulnerabilities Equities Process (VEP), meant to provide a balanced approach to this dilemma, lacks legal basis, oversight, and transparency. It would be prudent for Congress to regulate the process through a set of standards that require reporting and independent evaluation and ensure disclosure as the default standard. The utility of exploiting vulnerabilities decreases with time due to system updates, while the risk remains constant as long as there are vulnerabilities present.

The notion that ransomware is a relentless force of nature is misleading. This is actually a problem of poor policymaking. The burden of this problem falls on many people who have to bear the associated expenses. However, addressing it would require focused political action that can be challenging. However, it is possible since there is the required expertise.
